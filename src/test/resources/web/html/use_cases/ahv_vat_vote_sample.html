Financing the 13th AHV pension by a higher VAT: the vote of 29 November 2026

... more

The Swiss electorate decides on the federal decree on the additional financing of the AHV by an increase of the value added tax. The 13th AHV pension, accepted by 58 % of the voters in March 2024, is paid for the first time in December 2026 to 2.6 million pensioners whatever the outcome; it costs 4.2 billion CHF in 2026 and more than 5 billion CHF a year from 2035. Parliament decided in June 2026 to cover part of it by raising the normal VAT rate by 0.4 percentage points from 8.1 to 8.5 %, the hotel rate from 3.8 to 4.0 % and leaving the reduced rate of 2.6 % on food, medicines, books and newspapers unchanged; the Federal Council had asked for 0.7 points and the Council of States for 0.4 points plus 0.2 points of payroll contributions, a cut of the federal contribution was rejected by both chambers [1][4]. A VAT change amends the constitution, so the people and the cantons vote. The increase would apply from 2028 at the earliest and bring the AHV about 1.4 billion CHF a year from 2029, with the federal contribution roughly half of the cost of the 13th pension [1][2]. The Federal Council, the National Council (108 to 85) and the Council of States (28 to 13), the SP, the Greens, the Centre, the GLP, the EVP and the unions' federation SGB say yes; the FDP, the SVP, the employers, the trades and the public service union VPOD say no; the first polls put the opponents slightly ahead [6][8][9][10]. Below are the arguments of both sides with their numbers, then a calculator: say what you spend and earn, say how a no would be paid for instead, and see whether the VAT or the alternative costs your household more - and what that suggests for your vote.

Arguments for the VAT increase (yes)

  • The people decided, the pension must be paid for Without new revenue the AHV's pay-as-you-go deficit grows from more than 1 billion CHF in 2026 to almost 5 billion CHF in 2035; the VAT halves the deficit of 2030 from 2.7 to 1.2 billion CHF and keeps the fund at about 70 % of a year's expenditure in 2035 instead of 50 %. [1][2]
... more
  • All generations pay, pensioners included Payroll contributions would fall on the working population alone; the VAT reaches consumption, so pensioner households contribute 7 to 25 CHF a month, and tourists pay too. The Federal Council calls it the fairest base. [5][13]
  • Moderate and socially balanced The Federal Tax Administration puts the extra VAT at 15 CHF a month for the average household, 7 CHF below 4'600 CHF of income and 28 CHF above 13'400 CHF; food, medicines and women's hygiene products stay at 2.6 %. [5]
  • Money still flows from top to bottom Nine of ten people get more out of the AHV than they pay in; high earners consume more and so pay more VAT, but never receive more than the maximum pension of 2'520 CHF a month. [5][8]
  • A no leaves the gap and invites a higher retirement age The 4 to 5 billion CHF a year stay unfunded either way; the SGB warns that the opponents aim at a higher retirement age for all, the next reform AHV 2030 reaches Parliament in spring 2027. [1][8]
  • A compromise with broad backing Parliament settled on the minimum after two years: no payroll contributions, no cut of the federal contribution, 0.4 instead of 0.7 points; a cheap tax to collect that Switzerland has used for the AHV since 1999. [4][6]

Arguments against the VAT increase (no)

  • Reform the AHV instead of raising taxes The most convincing argument in the polls: the gaps of the AHV should be closed by structural reform, not by taxes and levies. The FDP's model: if the fund falls below 90 %, the VAT rises by 0.5 points and the reference age by six months; the Confederation's 90 billion CHF a year must suffice. [5][10][12]
... more
  • Less than half, and for ever The 1.4 to 1.5 billion CHF cover a third of the 4.2 to 5.4 billion CHF, the increase is unlimited in time and not tied to any reform; the FDP speaks of a "VAT swindle" and salami tactics, the AHV 2030 scenarios already contain the next increase. [4][7]
  • Purchasing power and the economy Households already carry rising premiums, rents and prices; BAK Economics for the FDP puts the cost at 545 CHF per household and year and the loss of output at 3.6 billion CHF after ten years, with retail, restaurants and SMEs hit hardest and shopping tourism of more than 10 billion CHF a year growing. [7][16]
  • Regressive, not solidary Low-income households spend a larger share of their income, so the VAT weighs more on them; the public service union VPOD and the left wing wanted payroll contributions, which rise with the wage. [6][9][12]
  • The AHV is not in need The fund holds about 45 billion CHF, earned 6.3 % in 2025 and made more than 10 billion CHF in two years; the VPOD calls it a record and sees no urgency. [9][11]
  • Farmers and employers Many farms do not settle VAT and bear the higher input cost themselves; the employers' association warns of the undesired macroeconomic effects of a permanent VAT increase. [5][15]

Formulas and sources

extra VAT revenue formula the money the AHV gets from the increase: the increase in points times the revenue per point of the normal rate

replacement formula after a no the same amount has to come from somewhere: the share replaced by payroll contributions and the share replaced from the federal budget are set by the reader, the rest stays unfunded and is taken from the fund; if the two shares add up to more than 100 %, the federal share is cut to the remainder

payroll points formula the contribution rate needed for the payroll share: the replaced amount divided by the revenue per point of contributions

extra VAT formula what your household pays: the monthly spending at the normal rate times twelve, times the increase in points divided by 108.1 (the increase is applied to the net price, the spending includes today's 8.1 %), times the pass-through to prices

payroll cost formula the wage income times twelve, times the payroll points divided by 100, times the share of the contribution that falls on the employee's pocket

federal cost formula the amount replaced from the federal budget in billion CHF times the household's share of a billion of federal savings

deferred cost formula the unfunded amount divided by the number of households, times the weight the reader gives to a franc missing in the fund; billion CHF divided by million households gives thousand CHF

household cost difference formula the annual cost of the yes path (extra VAT plus indirect cost) minus the annual cost of the no path (payroll cost plus federal cost plus deferred cost); a negative difference means the VAT is the cheaper way for your household

coverage formula the share of the 13th pension that is paid for: the federal contribution (the federal share of the AHV expenditure times the cost of the 13th pension) plus the new revenue, divided by the cost of the 13th pension

deficit formula the pay-as-you-go deficit of 2030 without extra financing minus the extra VAT revenue (yes) or minus the replacement revenue if it has started by 2030 (no); the replacement starts 2028 plus the years of delay

fund formula the fund of 2035 with the VAT is the BSV figure; without it the BSV figure without extra financing plus the replacement revenue times the years it has flowed until 2035, divided by the expenditure of 2035

generation formula the share of the new revenue paid by pensioner households: their share of normal-rate consumption for the VAT and for the federal budget, zero for payroll contributions; the unfunded part is left out because nobody has paid it yet

break-even spending formula the normal-rate spending at which the cost difference is zero: the cost of the no path minus the indirect cost, divided by twelve times the increase over 108.1 times the pass-through

verdict formula yes if the cost difference is below minus the threshold, no if it is above plus the threshold, undecided in between; the threshold is an assumption of the reader

model limits one year of operation around 2030 in prices of 2025, no growth of spending or wages, no hotel rate, no VAT paid indirectly through businesses that cannot deduct it (banks, insurers, hospitals, the public sector), no investment return of the fund, no behavioural change of consumers, no structural reform as a way of a no (a higher retirement age is a cost in years of work, not in francs); the BSV financial perspectives and the ESTV estimate are the places to look for these effects
