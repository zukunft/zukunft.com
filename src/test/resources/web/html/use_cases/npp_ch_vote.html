New nuclear power plants in Switzerland: the vote of 28 February 2027

The Swiss electorate decides whether the ban on building new nuclear power plants (NPP), in force since 2018, is lifted again. Parliament passed the amendment of the Nuclear Energy Act as the indirect counter-proposal to the "Blackout stoppen" initiative; the referendum against it was filed with about 132'000 signatures in October 2026 [1][2]. A yes only allows new plants; the build decision would follow in the 2030s and a plant would run from about 2050 [3][5]. Below are the arguments of both sides with their numbers, then a calculator: set your own assumptions with the sliders and see whether a new plant, under these assumptions, lowers or raises the average electricity cost in 2050 - and what that suggests for your vote.

Arguments for lifting the ban (yes)

  • Winter supply gap The four existing plants deliver 36 % of Swiss electricity and almost half of the winter electricity; the last one (Leibstadt) is expected to stop around 2044, while demand rises to about 76 TWh by 2050. Without new plants the winter net import grows to 7.5 TWh in 2050; one EPR cuts it to 3.2 TWh. [5]
  • Competitive if the construction cost stays moderate With state support and financing at 5 % instead of 8 %, two of four ETH/PSI models build 2 GW at 8'000 CHF/kW, and all four build 2.6 to 4.9 GW at 5'000 CHF/kW. [3]
  • Lower and steadier wholesale prices A new plant lowers the average wholesale price by 7 to 9 CHF/MWh and dampens winter peaks of 130 to 135 CHF/MWh; the avoided price volatility, outage and shortage risks are valued at about 520 million CHF per year. [5]
  • Generation cost in the range of renewables Levelised cost 2050 of a new plant 80 to 155 CHF/MWh versus 60 to 130 CHF/MWh for rooftop PV and 60 to 100 CHF/MWh for Swiss onshore wind - before the storage, backup and grid cost that intermittent sources add. [5]
  • Climate and land 10 to 20 g CO₂-eq/kWh over the life cycle against about 198 g for today's winter import mix; about 7'500 GWh per km² and year, 54 times the density of rooftop PV. [5]
  • Value added and jobs About 1.6 billion CHF value added and 2'900 full-time jobs per year over 60 years of operation (study commissioned by economiesuisse). [5]
  • An option, not an obligation Lifting the ban keeps the nuclear option open; whether a plant is built is decided later on cost and demand. [3][4]

Arguments for keeping the ban (no)

  • Not competitive under present conditions 19 energy experts of ETH Zurich and PSI, four models: new plants are too expensive without subsidies and state-borne financing risk, even at 5'000 to 8'000 CHF/kW. At 12'000 CHF/kW, the level of recent projects in Europe and the US, three of four models build none even with support. [3]
  • 14 to 43 billion CHF The Swiss Academies of Sciences put the cost of one plant at 14 billion CHF (Westinghouse AP1000) or 21 billion CHF (EDF EPR) in the optimistic case, 25 or 43 billion CHF if the overruns of Olkiluoto, Flamanville, Hinkley Point and Vogtle repeat. [4]
  • Permanent subsidies Even the pro study calculates a levelised cost of 115.5 CHF/MWh against a market price of 64 CHF/MWh: the market pays 55 %, the state about 625 million CHF per year. No Western new build of the last twenty years was financed without state support. [5]
  • Too late for the gap it is meant to close About 23 years from the vote to commercial operation (2050); the existing plants stop in the 2030s and 2040s. The head of the largest nuclear operator fears ten years of debate with nothing built. [5][8]
  • Net zero is reachable without All four models reach net zero 2050 with 36 to 43 TWh of solar and 0.3 to 8.6 TWh of wind; hydro and PV form three quarters of the supply. Winter imports stay in every scenario, with or without new plants. [3]
  • No cheaper electricity bill The models do not show a lower energy part of the tariff with new plants than with renewables; subsidies paid through the grid surcharge raise the bill. [6]
  • Accident, waste and cluster risk A residual risk and a single large unit (1.6 GW, about 16 % of demand) whose outage must be covered - the pro study leaves the risk side out; an insurance study prices the risk below 0.0001 Rp/kWh, the opponents call it uninsurable. [5][7]

Formulas and sources

annual production formula the electricity of the new plant: the capacity times the capacity factor times 8760 hours; GW times hours gives GWh, divided by 1000 TWh

investment formula the overnight construction cost per kW times the capacity, plus the interest during construction: the overnight cost times the cost of capital times the construction time times the interest-during-construction factor (the share of the construction time over which the capital is on average outstanding, 0.4 for an S-curve of the payments)

annuity factor formula the share of the investment to be recovered each year: r / (1 - (1 + r)^-n) with the cost of capital r and the lifetime n in years; the only formula of this page that needs a power

levelised cost formula the investment times the annuity factor divided by the annual production, plus the operating cost per MWh (personnel, maintenance, fuel, decommissioning fund and waste fund)

subsidy need formula what the market does not pay: the annual production times the levelised cost minus the market price, zero if the plant covers its cost; this is the amount a contract for difference or a grid surcharge would carry

alternative cost formula the cost of the same kilowatt-hours from the renewable alternative: the levelised cost of PV and wind plus the system cost of intermittent supply (storage, backup, grid)

shifted trade formula the nuclear output has a higher winter share than the renewable output, so the plant replaces winter imports and gives up summer exports of the same amount: the annual production times the difference of the winter shares is the shifted energy; its value is the shifted energy times the winter import price minus the shifted energy times the summer export price

winter net import formula the winter net import with the new plant: the winter net import without it minus the shifted energy

security value formula the value of the secured winter supply: the shifted energy times the value per MWh set by the user; zero if only the cost counts

cost difference formula the annual cost of the scenario with the new plant minus the scenario without: the production times the levelised cost, minus the production times the alternative cost, minus the value of the shifted trade, minus the security value; a negative difference means the new plant is cheaper

average cost difference formula the cost difference spread over all consumption: the annual cost difference divided by the electricity demand; million CHF per TWh equals 0.1 Rp per kWh

household price formula the energy tariff plus grid, metering and levies; the energy tariff with the new plant is the energy tariff without it plus the average cost difference; the bill is the price times 4'500 kWh

break-even construction cost formula the overnight cost at which the cost difference is zero: the levelised cost that makes both options equal (the alternative cost plus the trade and security value per nuclear MWh) minus the operating cost, divided by the capital cost per MWh of one CHF per kW

verdict formula yes if the average cost difference is below minus the threshold, no if it is above plus the threshold, undecided in between; the threshold is an assumption of the reader

model limits annual balance in prices of 2024 for one year of operation around 2050, no hourly dispatch, no learning or escalation of costs over time, no construction delay beyond the construction time set, no risk cost, no CO₂ price, no grid expansion difference between central and decentral supply; the ETH/PSI models and the BAK balance model are the places to look for these effects
